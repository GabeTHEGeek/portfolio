Building Fleet Command: from AI assistants to an operating system.

Why I stopped thinking about AI as a collection of assistants and started designing an operating layer for specialized agents, permissions, state, evidence, and autonomy.

The first version of the idea was simple: give different AI agents different jobs. One handles sales. One handles product. One handles engineering. At first that sounds like a multi-agent demo. The interesting problem starts when those agents have to coordinate.

Once the agents were doing different kinds of work, I ran into the same questions that show up inside real organizations. Who owns what? What information is shared? What requires approval? What happens when one agent finishes a task another agent depends on? What evidence has to exist before something can move forward?

Core idea The harder problem is not giving every agent more intelligence. It is designing the operating layer that lets specialized agents work together without creating chaos.

Assistants were the wrong frame.

Most AI products are still framed as assistants. The user asks for something. The model responds. The interaction is useful, but the human remains the center of every action.

That model breaks down once AI starts occupying persistent roles. A sales agent that finds prospects, scores them, drafts outreach, and tracks responses is not simply answering a prompt. It is participating in a workflow with state, dependencies, permissions, and outcomes.

Your next hire might not be human. The product question is how that hire fits into the organization.

That framing pushed Fleet Command away from “four agents in a UI” and toward something closer to an operating system.

The organization became the product.

The individual agents are important, but they are not the most interesting part of the system. The coordination layer is.

In Fleet Command, Sidious acts as the chief of staff. It holds the broader plan, routes work, tracks state, and surfaces decisions. Specialist agents then operate inside narrower responsibilities.

This created a much more useful design question: What does an AI organization need in order to operate reliably?

What the system needed.

I ended up thinking about the product around a few shared layers rather than around individual chat experiences.

1

State

Every agent needs to understand what is happening now, what has already happened, and what work is waiting.

2

Memory

The organization needs durable knowledge that survives a single conversation or agent run.

3

Permissions

Different agents should have different boundaries around what they can read, change, send, approve, or publish.

4

Evidence

Important actions should depend on verifiable inputs instead of model confidence alone.

5

Artifacts

The work itself needs to persist as specs, outreach, builds, reports, and other outputs that agents can pass between one another.

That is also where deterministic gates became useful. For example: no verified finding means no outreach. No approved scope means no build. No evidence means no client claim.

Designing for autonomy.

I do not think the end state is a human approving every individual AI action. If AI systems become reliable enough, organizations should be able to delegate larger areas of responsibility to them.

But autonomy is not a binary switch. The system needs a way to expand permissions as reliability increases.

Product principle Human oversight is useful as a control mechanism today. It should not become a permanent assumption about what these systems can eventually do.

That led to progressive autonomy levels, explicit permissions, logs, escalation, and rollback. The point is not to force a human into every loop. The point is to make the system understandable enough that responsibility can safely move away from the human when the evidence supports it.

What I learned building it.

The biggest lesson is that the future of agentic software may look less like a better chatbot and more like organizational infrastructure.

As soon as agents persist, specialize, share context, and take actions, product design starts overlapping with operating-model design. The questions become about responsibility, coordination, permissions, escalation, and trust.

That is the part of the space I find most interesting.

Fleet Command is still evolving.

I’m using it as a working product experiment around multi-agent coordination, progressive autonomy, and what it means to design software for an AI workforce instead of a single AI assistant.
